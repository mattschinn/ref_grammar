Translation exercise #2.

A.  BARE UTTERANCE

    1.  Test blurt adsorption

        a.  "I said it"

            - {I went, boom-boom-boom}, \<*Beunheumm rhentt,
              rhémmen·bèm*\>.

            - {I went, yakitty-yak.}, \<*Beunheumm rhentt, rekriiâ*\>*.*

            - *O*, from GA "to go", here featured in perfect aspect
              *rhentt*, from GA "went". The semantic origin is clearly
              the quotative use of "to go". Here, it means simply "to
              say". The quotative origin is clearly seen the
              intransitive use (there is no "it" object) and an
              obligatory ideophone follow up.

              - Notice that \< *beunheun* + *rent*\>, from GA "went
                ahead and went", goes through a diachronic path that
                includes a /nw/ cluster (boundary between *and* and
                *went*, with the former realized colloquially as *an'* ,
                with the voiced obstruent coda elided), which shifts to
                /m:/. Orthographically, the geminate m is shown as
                *beunheumm*, though *rhentt* keeps its spelling. This
                should touch on both orthography and morphophonology.

            - *Rekriiâ* (from GA "yakitty-yak", should override existing
              entry *rékriiyæ* in form, and add ideophone meaning) and
              *rhémmen·bèm* (from GA "boom, boom, boom"; the second
              "boom" is subsumed into a geminated *m*) are ideophones,
              describing speech acts. *Rekriiâ* describes speaking in
              dialect (gives it the GA exonym '*Rickety Yak*'), while
              *rhémm·bèm* suggests bullet-pointed argumentation,
              suggesting some confidence and/or confrontation. Another
              ideophone for speech act *rhitmmtm* (from GA "nuh-uh" and
              similar onomatopoetic variants; irregular diachronic path;
              stress on geminated syllabic *m*; both \<*t*\> are
              realized as glottal stops), describing negation or refusal
              or disagreement. *Bauwouwou* is the ideophone that
              describes speaking in General American.

              - Ideophones can leave at the edge of the phonological
                boundaries, featuring non-phonemes or rare phonemes, and
                breaking phonotactical constraints on ultimate syllable
                stress.

              - Ideophones can have hand motions associated with them,
                making the multimodal nature of everyday conversations
                more salient. *Rekriiâ* and *bauwouwou* has the hand
                rotating around the wrist (the "so on and so forth"
                motion) and *rhémm·bèm* has one hand rhythmically
                clapping the other with the back of the hand ("boom,
                boom, boom" motion).

        b.  "It slipped out / I blurted it out.": "Got myself going,
            yakitty-yak".

            - "*Rimmaśe oę,* *rekriiâ*."

            - Straightforwardly, this is the verb \<*o*\> in
              non-volitional frame.

    2.  Test infinitive-shaped vs progressive-shaped concord on one
        stem.

        a.  "I finally said it."

            - {Got myself to go, boom-boom-boom} \< *Rimmaśeto o,
              rhémmen·bèm* \>

              - Non-volitional frame with infinitive shape suggests
                great effort was made. The actual explicit word
                "finally" was omitted from the translation.

        b.  "I was saying it when she walked in".

            - {Got myself going boom-boom-boom, which is when they go
              ahead barge in} \< *Rimmaśe oę rhémmen·bèm, rhishisben ei
              retmseto baurzıĩ*\>

              - *Rhishisben*: from GA "which is when" and "which has
                been" converging and merging phonetically, serving
                similar functions -- linking two related clauses
                together. (1) the meaning from "which is when" is the
                show that clause 1 and clause 2 happen simultaneously or
                in quick succession. (2) Clause 2 describes clause 1
                with additional context or background from the recent
                past.\
                Instead of *rhishisben*, we could also use *kyii* (from
                GA "cue"), which links the two clauses describing
                successive events, but also implies that clause 2
                follows in response to clause 1 (a mild causal
                implication, if you will). The use of *kyii* here*,*
                then, would suggest irony and some sarcasm.

              - I used the non-volitional frame for "barge in" here, to
                give it actually a mild malefactive reading (the fact
                that she walked in then is inconvenient or undesirable).
                The semantic route, I presume, is that when the
                *get-oneself* construction carries an auto-benefactive
                reading for somebody else, it could imply (in a zero-sum
                kind of way) that this is malefactive for the speaker. I
                think this reading has not been documented before. I
                think it shows that the semantic colors imbued by the
                *get-oneself* frame is complex and varied. The
                alternative \<... *rhishisben ei ouheun baurzıĩ*\> is
                more neutral in tone.

              - *Baurzıĩ:* verb, from GA "to barge in". In dialect, it
                simply means to enter. As an interjection ("*baurzıĩ!"*)
                it is used as a welcoming greeting, or an invitation to
                come in (i.e., in response to knocking), comparable to
                GA "come on in!". GA "welcome", in its reduplicated form
                \<*belkmlkm*\> can also be used with that former
                greeting sense. In contrast, the non-duplicated form
                \<*belkm*\> carries the sense of "you are welcome".

        c.  NOTES

    3.  Test the tell-as-possession causative and the definite-object
        restriction with an abstract possessum.

        a.  "I told her the news":

            - {Went ahead and filled them in on it, the news},
              \<*beunheun þiltm·ę·aų·et, o rıĩs.*\>

              - All in all a complex verb conjugation marked by direct
                and indirect object, as well as perfect aspect form with
                the GA past suffix -*ed* being realized as glottal stop
                spelled \<*t*\>.

              - Note here "the news" is still definite. If a new
                information was being conveyed, it could be accommodated
                with a non-dislocated form: {went ahead and filled them
                in on a news}, \<*beunheun þiltm·ę·ą o riųś* \>. The
                expectation here is then that the speaker will explain
                what this "news" is. Note the form "a news", which is
                ungrammatical in GA (where it should be "some news", or
                simply "news") but is valid here by analogy.

            - {News-wise, went ahead and filled them in}, \< *Riųś*
              *·wiis, beunheun þiltm·en.\>*, alternative order
              \<*beunheun þiltm·en, riųś·wiis \>* is also valid.

              - Here, *wiis* (from GA "-wise"; used to mark backgrounded
                context. Compare "regarding", "when it comes to") is
                used to describe the object (definite, known, already
                introduced into discourse). Here, the likely flow of the
                conversation will be centered around the telling of the
                news.

        b.  "You know the news -- I told her":

            - {(you) know the news, went ahead and filled them in},
              \<*Nou riųś, beunheun þiltm·en\>*, note that here, the
              alternative order is not valid! *Nou riųś* needs to stay
              on the left side. The *know* construction directs the
              listener's attention to a new topic ("the news") that,
              nevertheless, the speaker is familiar with.

        c.  NOTES

            - The sentences above describe syntax considerations based
              on pragmatics and information structure. Topicality and
              new/old information drive it. Need to refine description
              of it.

            - *Þilnn:* from GA "to fill in" (stress on the syllabic
              geminated *n*). Verb. (1) Shares with GA "to tell" on the
              meaning, *to inform*. It does not carry many of the other
              meanings of "to tell" (e.g., to narrate or recount; notice
              or discern, especially in "can tell". These meanings are
              *not* carried by *þilnn*). Notice that with this meaning,
              the direct object marker is infixed between "*fill*" and
              "*in*", and the indirect object is suffixed onto it at the
              end with "*on*". When agglutinated, these strings of
              suffixes blend together, so that "fill them in on it"
              becomes *þilmm·ę·ą·et.* Other meanings include: (2) to
              replace, to take the stead of something else, to
              substitute (3) (*intrans*.) to discern by context or
              social cues, "to read the room", from GA "to fill in the
              blank", used similarly to "to read between the lines",
              used with \<*niblę*\> from GA "the blank" (irregular sound
              changes; the initial *n* comes from /-nð-/ cluster at the
              "in the" word boundary geminating to /-n:-/. The /æ/ is
              raised in front of a nasal, as is typical of GA; its short
              vowel quantity (rather than the expected long /ɛ:/) can be
              attributed to the fact that "the blank" is an unstressed
              part of a formulaic expression and therefore less fully
              enunciated.

    4.  Test talk versus speak.

        a.  We talked for hours.

            - {We got ourselves to yickitty-yack for a eon.}, \<*Rhi*
              *riaśeto* *rekriiâ þri·ei·iiǫ*\>

              - *þri·ei·iiǫ:* from GA "for a eon", adverb, meaning "for
                a long time".

        b.  I spoke with the manager.

            - {I went ahead and spoke with the manager.}, \<*Beunheun*
              *ospokwþ o bäeĩyi*\>

              - *Ospii*: from GA "to speak". Notice the epenthetic \<o\>
                in front. Here, it takes the suffix *wþ* (from GA
                "with"), which preserves the original -*k* coda as *kw*.

              - *Bäeĩyi:* from GA "manager".

        c.  The test aims to compare *talk* and *speak*, but the verbs
            share a large semantic space and diverge on specific use
            cases (I *speak* English, I'm *speaking* at the seminar) and
            phrases (*talk* someone into or out of, *talk* over, to
            *talk* the *talk*; *speak* up). It is unclear what aspect of
            *talk* versus *speak* it is trying to test.\
            From the example sentences, the test seems to be pointing
            towards how *talking* suggests an informal chat while
            *speaking* suggests a formal discussion. This semantic
            distinction is rather weak though (compare "we talked for
            hours" versus "we spoke for hours"; "I spoke with the
            manager" versus "I talked to the manager". The difference in
            meaning seems minimal).\
            Here, the verb choice ("yack") suggests informal chatter,
            the non-volitional frame highlights a lack of control (of
            how fast time flew) or intention (they did not mean to
            chatter for hours, but it was so engaging that it happened
            so), and the infinitive-shape suggests the chatter has ended
            (perfect aspect).\
            For the second sentence, the verb *to speak* is preserved.
            The volitional frame is appropriate for a deliberate
            conversation that are often goal-oriented. The perfect
            aspect is used to signal the event is over.

5.  Compare general hearsay versus personal source.

    a.  "They say the bridge is out." / {(You) know the bridge, (I)
        guess it.does not.quite run really } / \< *Nou baurrits, reś eos
        noquii rin riiy.* \>

    b.  "I heard the bridge is out." / {(You) know the bridge, (I) guess
        it.does, quote, not.quite run really } / \< *Nou baurrits, reś
        et quo nokw rin riiy.* \>

    c.  [notes]{.smallcaps}

        - The matrix particle *reś*, from GA "(I) guess (that)", is used
          for indirect evidentiality in general. *Reś* does not change
          form with tense (the phrase "I guess ..." is anchored with the
          speech event itself, the 'now'). *Reś* should supersede and
          replace *piies* (from GA "(it) appears").

        - Personal source is marked by *quo*, from GA "quote", a kind of
          spoken punctuation. The position of *quo* should precede the
          topical, salient part of the quote. Here, it is positioned in
          front of *noquii,* the primary negation marker -- this
          communicates that the most salient part of the hearsay is that
          the bridge does *not*, in fact, run.

          - A flexible position for *quo* is after *reś* where it can
            negate the whole clause. In such case, the two lexemes merge
            as *resquo*: *Resquo nokw rin riiy*.

          - The position of *quo* can highlight doubt on the part of the
            speaker. *Reś eia ounaheun quo beussno* {Guess they are
            going ahead and, quote, passing out} suggests "they say
            they'll be going to bed (but who knows!)".

        - There is negation featured. Might clash with existing
          descriptions (for example, here, a reduced form of *nocquii*
          is used, in *nokw*). Need to flesh out and refine (how does it
          concord with weak pronomial clitic and verb shape).

          - The hand motion for this *not-quite* negation, which has a
            non-volitional reading that contrasts with the volitional
            negation *skip*, is "air quotes", with two fingers
            'flickering', either throughout the clause or at the end,
            with *riiy*, from GA "really". The "air quotes" can also go
            with *quo*, where it colors the clause with more doubt.

7.  Auditory versus visual inference.

    a.  "It sounds like rain."

    b.  "It looks like rain."

    c.  [notes]{.smallcaps}

        - Both sentences could be translated as {(I) guess it's going
          ahead and raining.} \< *Reś ets ounohen rheinn* \> or {I guess
          upon us (is) rain} \< *Reś pons o rheĩ*\> with ambiguity
          whether the evidence is sight-based or auditory. This mirrors
          how an active-voice paraphrasing ("I see/hear rain") would be
          similarly ambiguous: {Got myself noticing the rain} \<
          *rimmase* *nouśę o rheĩ* \>.

          - Worth unpacking. A neutral way of saying "there is ..."
            (cf., French *il y a*, Spanish *hay*, German *es gibt*) is
            *pons*, from GA "(there is) upon us". The form suggests
            irregular diachronic development -- we would expect the
            initial /p/ to voice, medial -*n*- to elide out while
            nasalizing the vowel, the schwa to smoothen out and lengthen
            the preceding vowel, and finally the coda /s/ to stay
            devoiced -- yielding expected form \**baųś*. Instead, /p/
            stays as is (not entirely irregular), /ɑ/ shifts to /ɔ/ then
            to /o/ (not entirely irregular), /n/ stays while the schwa
            drops, and /s/ voices to /z/, perhaps due to new proximity
            to /n/. Could be explained that grammatical words can
            develop in quirky ways.

          - In contrast to the neutral *pons* construction (*pons
            rheĩ*), constructions with *to rain* as a verb necessitates
            a volitional frame, with both frames providing semantic
            nuance. Assigning volition to the inanimate weather ("it's
            going ahead and raining (anyway)") suggests inconvenience or
            fatefulness, while suggesting lack of control to weather
            suggests unexpectedness ("it got itself to (actually)
            rain"). The actual nuance probably depends on context and
            the verb. While weather is the (common) topic at hand where
            inanimacy and volition meet in salient ways, this should be
            generally true: casting inanimate beings as active with
            either frame will likely have non-neutral semantic color.
            The grammar then invites impersonal or passive constructions
            for inanimate objects, and reserves their active framing for
            discourse effects. This should probably be further explored
            and refined with additional translation exercises.

        - The ambiguity above is often left to be resolved by context
          (*rickety yak* is a language between intimates). They could
          alternatively be resolved by:

          - Onomatopoeia and ideophones (notice the use of -*ðs*, from
            GA "this", to introduce onomatopoeia as an object).

            - \< *rimmase* *nouśnðs riprprp \>* { got myself
              noticing-this drip-drip-drip}

            - \< *rimmase* *nouśnðs bet bät bet bät \>* { got myself
              noticing-this pitter-patter pitter-patter}

          - Using visibility-based topic marker

            - \<*hii ossii,* *pons rheĩ* \> {see outside, rain (is)
              upon-us}

            - \<*no ossii,* *pons rheĩ* \> {(you) know outside, rain
              (is) upon-us}

8.  Edge test of the membership criterion.

    a.  "Word is they're hiring."

        - "Word is" does not beget a matrix particle. Matrix particles
          don't necessarily have to come from finite verb forms, nor do
          they have to be inflected for tense. I think their defining
          characteristics are (1) they operate at the clause-level, and
          relatedly, (2) their word order is fixed to clause-initial.
          Thus, it is their syntactical behavior that defines them.\
          So, the word *meiyi* can color the whole clause (*meiyi ei
          beunheun split*), but by adjusting the word order, can also
          just modify a part (*ei beunheun meiyi split)*. Similarly,
          words like *bauyiis* and *bauyen* or *quo* can appear flexibly
          in various word orders.\
          In contrast, matrix particles like *no* and *reś* need to
          appear at the beginning of the clause. Moreover, they don't
          need to inflect for tense. In cases like *reś*, "(I) guess"
          and "(I) guessed" converge phonologically so there is no
          surface form difference anymore. Furthermore, the phrase "I
          guess" is anchored firmly with the speech event (the speaker
          is conjecturing at that moment, whether about a past or
          present event) that inflection for tense does not make sense.

        - Consider the matrix particle *nóuśshè*, from GA phrase "in a
          nutshell", which marks the clause as a paraphrasis or summary,
          allowing the speaker to provide gist with caveat that there
          may be additional detail. Compare Italian *insomma*. {
          *Nóuśshè eia ounohen rhikriitm* } means "long story short,
          they are hiring" or "what is important is that they are
          hiring, although there are other relevant details". The
          provenance of the particle is adverbial ("in a nutshell"), but
          due to fixed phrasal pattern in GA, it's function and word
          order is rather fixed ("they are recruiting, in a nutshell" or
          "they are, in a nutshell, recruiting" are grammatical and
          understandable but awkward.)

        - In contrast, discourse-salient adverbial counterparts are
          *rhiksse*, from GA phrase "you could say", and *beiskiiy*,
          from GA phrase "basically", and *noê*, from GA phrase "in a
          way". They all mean "in a way, sort of, kind of" but with
          different colors of speaker attitude. Probably need a separate
          session with translation exercises to tease out nuance.

9.  Quotative vs plain

    a.  She was like, 'no way'.

        - {they go, no way} \<*ei o noê*\>

        - {they go, quote, "oh, no way"} \<*ei o quo o noê*\>

        - Notice \<*o*\> (from GA "go") to mean "to say", quotative
          \<*quo*\> (from GA "quote"), and reported speech marker
          \<*o*\> (from GA "oh"). The first is obligatory, the latter
          two are optionally used to clarify what context may leave out.

    b.  She said no.

        - {they go, (not) really} \<*ei o, noriiy*\> or \<*ei o,
          riiy*\>, but similar to above, also \<*ei o quo o noriiy*\>.
          Alternatively, an ideophone is used: \<*ei o,* *rhitmmtm\>*

10. Stance-quotation inheritance.

    a.  "I was like, finally!" (internal reaction, nothing uttered)\
        {On my end, I go, quote, 'finally'}\
        \< *Bauyen, e o quo 'þiiaii'* \>\
        Having the *quote* particle (preceding the quoted remark itself)
        on one's own thought suggests it as a thought. The strong
        pronoun in front, left-dislocated, was left there because it

        The *quote-oneself* approach obviously doesn't quite work with

    b.  I said, 'finally'.\

17. Question embedding.

    a.  "She asked if I was coming" {Got themselves to wonder, am-I
        going ahead and making it at all} \<*ritmseto rhinnw, emii
        ounohen meiknet ea*\>

    b.  "She asked, 'coming at all?'" {They go, oh hey, making it at
        all? } \<*ei o, ohei meikntt ea*\>

    c.  [notes]{.smallcaps}

        i.  *Rhinnw*, verb, from GA "to wonder", semantically shifted to
            mean "to ask, pose a question, to inquire" -- specifically
            with the *get-oneself* frame. In contrast, with the
            *go-ahead* frame, it means "to doubt, to be skeptical, to
            question".

        ii. Notice the inverted form *emii* used here with \<*ea*\> ("at
            all") for conditional meaning ("if, whether"). The form,
            though, is identical to interrogative ("am I going to make
            it at all?") -- the difference here being that the question
            will have a rising tone at the end.

        iii. The second sentence uses an interrogative reported speech
             marker / direct question maker \<*ohei*\> from GA "oh,
             hey", along with a clipped form ("making it at all?")
             allowed in questions (compare {(do you want a) morning joe
             at all?} \< *bǫëiio ea*?\> to mean, "would you like some
             coffee?").
